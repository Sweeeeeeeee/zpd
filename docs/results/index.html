Research question · Olympiad geometry

Can a scanner find an easy proof?

A geometry problem can have several proofs. We built a scanner to look for one that takes fewer steps, uses familiar rules, or needs just one extra point in the diagram.

It starts from the points and assumptions in a formalized problem. If deduction stalls, a heuristic or a trained model proposes a construction and the solver tries again. We compare the resulting proofs to see which rules and points actually helped.

A shorter hexagon proof

With all rules available, the solver used similarity and ratios. Turning off similarity led it to triangle congruence. Corresponding sides then gave both required equalities. Both routes used the original points.

Two useful auxiliary points

With circle rules and algebra, a heuristic found a circumcenter and the model proposed a midpoint. Each point let deduction solve one more problem. Both proofs could be repeated by giving the solver the point at the start.

Most problems stayed open

Across 1,170 original runs, 21 problems had a proof. With all rules and algebra, deduction alone solved 20. Searching for extra points added no wins in that setting. A later solver fix brought overall coverage to 22.

What counts as “easy”?

We can count deductions, name the rules used, and check whether a proof needed another point. Human difficulty is harder to measure. Only two Latvian problems had matching student scores. On one, the revised solver timed out even though students scored higher on it than on any other problem in that round. See the student comparison.

Latvian problems, proofs and rule comparisons

Of 80 candidates, 57 translations passed the automatic acceptance checks. Recorded decisions accepted another 8 and rejected 5, bringing total acceptances to 65. Review notes for the 10 unresolved cases and the six sampled translations include a corrected contest grade. The six selected translations satisfied every goal in 186 drawings out of 192 attempts. Independent human review remains open. A formalization can supply helper points before search begins. Reported search depth counts points added afterwards. The scan covers these accepted translations. A larger independently reviewed sample with matched student scores is needed to test how solver coverage relates to human difficulty.


We tried 64 fresh drawings for each of the 65 accepted translations. The engine constructed each figure before seeing its goals. Across 4,160 attempts, 3,940 satisfied every goal, 220 could not be built, 0 had coincident points within tolerance and 0 failed a goal. For the 1999 two-circle problem, the original drawing could place M outside segment AB. Restricting M to the segment increased successful drawings from 32 to 62 on a further 64 seeds. Drawings and construction comparison.


What did auxiliary-point search add?


We compared three search policies on each accepted translation, with algebraic reasoning (AR) on and off. Across 390 runs the 70 geometric rules, 60-second search budget and 2,048 MiB memory limit stayed fixed. The combined policy used Geo-LM mid and heuristics.

What changed when algebraic reasoning was switched off?


With the same 70 rules and no search for extra points, the original solver proved 20 of 65 problems with algebraic reasoning on and 0 of 65 with it off. We repeated both settings on all 20 original successes after the merge fix. The revised solver proved 19 with algebraic reasoning on and 0 with it off. In a separate 180-second retest of the case that timed out with algebraic reasoning on, it proved 1 with it on and 0 with it off. Extended-budget records.


Can a reader reconstruct the printed proof?


Across the 20 original successes, 14 of 48 algebraic steps followed from the equations cited in the step itself. Adding explicit equations from the original construction explained another 10. The remaining 24 need further geometric facts or reasoning.


In the grade 9 hexagon problem, the solver prints AE/BD = EF/BC and concludes EF = BC. The missing equality is AE = BD. The official solution proves it from a parallelogram that the formalization already supplies. Official solution, page 8 · Five official comparisons, paired runs and step-by-step checks.


What changed when similarity rules were removed?


Keeping algebraic reasoning on, deduction proved 15 of 65 problems with the 59 remaining rules. The full set proved all of these. For 12 problems the transcripts matched byte for byte. In the other 3, the original proof had used a removed rule and the restricted solver found a different argument.


In the grade 9 hexagon problem, the original seven steps used triangle similarity. The five-step restricted proof uses two angle equalities and the construction fact AE = BD to establish triangle congruence. Corresponding sides then give both goals. The two printed angle-chasing steps in the restricted proof follow from their cited equations. One other full-rule proof used only retained rules yet timed out in the restricted run. With a 180-second budget, the reduced solver reproduced that exact 21-step transcript. The other paired timeout exhausted its rules on the longer run. Matched outcomes and both transcripts.
